Here's a pattern you'll recognize. The designer finishes a gorgeous flow. Engineering says it's three sprints of work. Product says the deadline is in two. A VP who saw an early sketch has opinions. Nobody is wrong, and nothing ships the way it was drawn.

No design file can fix that. Only collaboration, expectations and decisions can.

As you grow, your impact depends less on how well you draw and more on how well you get good decisions made across an organization, including decisions in rooms you're not in. This chapter covers working across functions, managing stakeholders, mentoring others and deciding as a group, plus what's changing as AI reshapes teams.

:::pull
Influence isn't authority. Most of the time you have the first and not the second, and that's a skill you can learn.
:::

## 1. Working across functions

### Who you work with

| Partner | What they care about | What helps them |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Product manager | Outcomes, priorities, timelines, stakeholder demands | Evidence, trade-offs, clear recommendations |
| Engineer | Feasibility, quality, maintainability, estimates | Early involvement, clear specs, realistic scope, respect for constraints |
| Researcher | Rigor, evidence, impact | Time and access to users, shared questions |
| Content designer | Clarity, voice, consistency | Involvement before layout is fixed |
| Data analyst | Valid measurement | Clear questions and defined events |
| Legal, security | Risk | Early notice and plain explanations |
| Support, sales | Customer reality | Being heard, and seeing that feedback matters |

### Principles that make it work

- **Involve people early.** The best time to discover a technical constraint is *before* you design around it.
- **Share the problem, not only the solution.** People who understand why solve it better than people handed a spec.
- **Make decisions visible.** Where is the decision recorded, and who made it?
- **Define decision rights.** Disagreement is more manageable when it's clear whose call it is.
- **Work in the open.** Share work in progress. It builds trust and catches problems early.
- **Ask about constraints.** "What makes this hard?" gets further than "why can't we?"

### The engineer relationship

A strong designer–engineer relationship is often the biggest single lever on the quality of what ships. What helps:

- **Pair early** on feasibility and approach, before the design is locked.
- **Learn the stack** well enough to discuss constraints (see [[ch:10]]).
- **Agree what "done" means,** including states, accessibility and responsive behavior.
- **Review the build together** and treat differences as shared issues, not blame.
- **Make trade-offs explicit.** If something has to be cut, decide together what the user loses.

:::framework A working agreement
Write a short agreement on how you work together: how and when each discipline gets involved; where decisions are recorded; how design and engineering review work in progress; what "ready to build" and "done" mean; how you handle disagreement and escalation; how you use AI tools. Revisit it every quarter.
:::

### 2026 shifts

- **Roles are blurring.** Designers prototype in code, engineers use AI for interface work, PMs generate prototypes. One secondary survey summary says many designers do more work overlapping with product and engineering, and Figma's State of the Designer 2026, as relayed through secondary coverage, reports designers' participation in development doubling in a year. Neither figure is verified here.[[ref:musemind-ai-ux]][[ref:figma-sotd-2026]] The practical implication is clarity about who owns the quality of what: when anyone can build a prototype, who's accountable for the shipped result?
- **Smaller teams do more.** NN/g reports team sizes holding steady or possibly growing, but more asked of each role.[[ref:nng-state-ux-2026]] Breadth helps, but depth in your craft is why you're trusted.
- **Agree how the team uses AI:** who uses which tools, what data may go into them, how generated work is reviewed and who's accountable. Decide it together, not alone.

:::exercise Try it: a working agreement (1 hour)
Draft a one-page agreement covering involvement, decisions, done, disagreement and AI use. Share it and ask each partner what they'd change. Revisit in a month.
:::

## 2. Stakeholder management

Stakeholders are anyone who can affect or is affected by your work: executives, product leaders, sales, support, legal, other teams. They differ in power (can they stop the work?), interest (do they care?) and incentives (what are they measured on?).

### A simple method

1. **Map them.** List who they are, their interest, influence and what they're measured on. Who decides? Who must be consulted? Who needs to be informed?
2. **Understand their problem.** A feature request is usually a solution to a problem you haven't heard yet.
3. **Set expectations early and often.** Surprise is what damages trust.
4. **Bring evidence and options,** not opinion contests.
5. **Give people a role** at the right moment and explain how their input was used.
6. **Close the loop,** especially when you didn't take their suggestion.

### Saying no, well

A pattern that works: **acknowledge the goal** ("You want to cut the time people spend on this report"), **state the trade-off** ("All twelve filters would delay release by three weeks and make the page harder to scan"), **offer an alternative** ("Launch with the three most used and measure the rest"), and **say what you need** ("If you want all twelve, which other item do we drop?").

### Conflict and loud voices

- **Separate the person from the position** and look for the concern behind a demand.
- **Escalate by deciding who decides,** not by going around people. If two stakeholders disagree, bring them together with the evidence and a recommendation, or ask the decision owner.
- **Use shared goals:** "We both want fewer support tickets; here's what the data says."
- **Disagree and commit.** Once decided, support it, and record your concern so the team can learn from the result.

:::warning Common trap: surprising people in public
Introducing a major change for the first time in a big meeting forces defensive reactions. Pre-wire: talk to key people one-to-one first so the meeting is for deciding, not first reactions.
:::

:::exercise Try it: map stakeholders for a project (1 hour)
List every stakeholder, what they're measured on, what they want, their influence and current support. Pick the two most important relationships and plan one conversation for each: what you'll ask and what you'll offer.
:::

## 3. Mentoring

Mentoring is helping someone grow faster than they would alone. It differs from managing (which includes performance and accountability) but uses similar skills, and at senior levels it's part of the expectation.

### Doing it well

- **Start with their goals.** What do they want to be able to do in a year?
- **Coach more than you advise.** Ask questions that help them think ("What are you noticing? What options do you see?"). Offer your view when asked or when it saves real pain.
- **Give specific, timely feedback** in the structures from [[ch:07]].
- **Teach judgment, not just steps.** Explain why you chose something and what you looked at.
- **Stretch with support.** Give a bit more scope than is comfortable, and stay available.
- **Share your mistakes.** It makes learning normal.
- **Make a growth plan** with two or three concrete goals, reviewed monthly.

When you delegate, define the outcome, constraints and check-in points, not the method. Resist taking the work back when it's not done your way.

### 2026 shifts

- **Entry-level roles are scarce.** NN/g's State of UX 2026 says senior and generalist roles are recovering faster than entry-level positions, which stay scarce and competitive.[[ref:nng-state-ux-2026]] That makes every junior designer's development more valuable.
- **AI is a shortcut and a risk for learning.** A junior can produce plausible work without understanding why it works. Ask them to explain decisions, critique generated options against stated criteria (see [[ch:01]] and [[ch:12]]) and do some work the slow way to build the underlying skill.
- **Teach what AI doesn't replace:** problem framing, research, critical thinking, communication and taste. Pair juniors on research and stakeholder conversations, not just production.
- **Mentor both ways.** Juniors are often more fluent with new tools. Learn from them, and say so.

:::exercise Try it: a growth conversation (1 hour)
Meet a colleague who wants to grow. Ask what they want to be doing in a year, what feels hard and what they want from you. Agree two goals and a monthly review. Afterwards note when you were coaching and when you were advising.
:::

## 4. Group decisions

[[ch:07]] covers facilitation as a craft. In leadership, the question is *how decisions actually get made.*

### Decision rights

Most friction comes from not knowing who decides. Frameworks like DACI (driver, approver, contributors, informed) and RAPID give a vocabulary, and their value is forcing the team to name roles, not the acronym. Before any significant decision: name the decider, state the rule, set a deadline, record the outcome and reasoning.

:::decision Choosing a decision rule
Use **consensus** rarely, for decisions that need everyone's full support. Use **consent** (anyone can object with a reason; otherwise we proceed) for most team decisions. Use **a named decider after input** when speed matters or responsibility is clear. Use a **vote** to prioritize many options, not to settle contested principles.
:::

### Decision records

A short written record (context, options, choice, reasons, date) saves repeated debate, helps new people understand why things are the way they are and protects you when a choice is questioned later.

### Leading without authority

You can lead a decision without being the manager: **frame the problem** so others see it the same way, **prepare the options** and trade-offs, **facilitate** so quieter perspectives are heard, **propose a recommendation** and ask for objections, and **follow through** (write it up, assign next steps, check progress).

Growth can mean deeper craft and wider influence (senior, staff, principal) or leading people (manager, director). They're different jobs, so try the early parts of each (mentoring, leading a project, running a team ritual) before choosing.

### 2026 shifts

- **Decisions move faster with less context.** With generated options and summaries it's tempting to decide quickly on plausible material. Slow down for high-impact or hard-to-reverse choices and check the evidence.
- **Accountability for AI-assisted decisions stays with people.** If a tool proposed it and a person approved it, the person owns it. Record what the tool was asked and what you verified.

:::exercise Try it: write a decision record (30 minutes)
Take a recent product decision. Write half a page: context, options, choice, reasons, who decided and the date. Ask someone who wasn't involved whether they could explain the decision from it alone.
:::

## Case: a disagreement, resolved

Here's a situation you'll live through several times a year. You've designed an inline-editing table. The engineering lead says it's too expensive for this release and proposes a modal editor instead. You think the modal will hurt the core workflow. The conversation gets tense.

**What not to do.** Defend the design harder, escalate to the PM, or quietly redesign around the engineer's back.

**A better path.**

1. **Get curious about the constraint.** "Help me understand what makes inline editing expensive here." You learn the data layer can't validate per cell, so each edit would need custom plumbing, about nine days.
2. **Restate the shared goal.** "We both want people to update many records quickly without errors."
3. **Separate the need from the solution.** The need: fast, low-error bulk edits. Inline editing is one way. A modal is another. Are there others?
4. **Generate options together.** (A) Modal editor, 2 days, slower for bulk edits. (B) Inline editing, 9 days. (C) Inline editing for the two most-edited columns only, 4 days, modal for the rest. (D) Bulk edit panel with multi-select, 5 days.
5. **Use evidence.** From research: 70 percent of edits touch two columns. That makes C attractive.
6. **Decide with a clear rule.** The PM, as decider, picks C after hearing both sides. You commit.
7. **Record it.** A short decision note: context, options, choice, reasons, plus "revisit after we see usage data in six weeks."
8. **Close the loop.** Share the result with the engineer. Credit the idea that came from the conversation.

The outcome is better than either starting position, and the relationship is stronger. That's the pattern: curiosity, shared goal, options, evidence, a clear decider and a record.

## A script for saying no

> "Thanks for flagging this. I want to make sure I understand the goal: [restate their goal]. Here's what I see: adding all twelve filters would push the release by about three weeks and make the page harder to scan for most people. What I'd suggest is launching with the three filters that cover most use, measuring the rest and adding them if the data shows demand. If twelve is a hard requirement, tell me which other item we should defer, and I'll update the plan."

Why it works: it opens with the goal, states a concrete trade-off, offers a path, and puts the choice back with the person who owns it.

## A growth conversation

Use this for a 45-minute one-to-one with a designer you mentor:

| Segment | Prompts |
| --- | --- |
| Where you are (10 min) | "What's going well? What's frustrating? What are you proud of lately?" |
| Where you want to go (10 min) | "In a year, what do you want to be doing? What would make you feel you've grown?" |
| Gaps and options (15 min) | "What skills would get you there? What opportunities exist on current projects?" |
| Commitments (10 min) | "Two goals for the next quarter. What support do you need from me? When do we check in?" |

Afterward, write a short summary and send it. The act of writing makes it real.

## Team AI norms

If your team hasn't talked about AI use, start with a one-pager:

- **Allowed tools:** which are approved, and for what kinds of work.
- **Data rules:** what may never be pasted (customer data, unreleased plans, personal information).
- **Review rules:** generated work is reviewed like anyone's work, and the person who ships it owns it.
- **Disclosure:** when we say AI was used (client work, research, imagery).
- **Learning:** we share what worked and what failed, monthly.
- **Review date:** we revisit this in three months, because tools change.

## Where to go deeper

- **Difficult conversations:** state the facts, the impact, your intent and an invitation.
- **Feedback to people more senior than you:** frame around shared goals, be specific.
- **Managing up:** make your manager's job easier with clear priorities, risks and asks.
- **Design operations:** the rituals, tools and processes that help a team run smoothly, including how AI tools are chosen and governed.
- **Inclusion in teams:** whose voice isn't being heard, and what could change that.

## Quick check

:::check Knowledge check 1
A senior executive asks for a feature you think is wrong. What do you do?
---
Find the goal behind the request, share your evidence, and show options and trade-offs including what the feature costs in time and in the experience. Offer an alternative that serves their goal and a small test to learn more. If the decision goes against your advice, disagree and commit, record your concern and agree how you'll measure the result so the team learns either way.
:::

:::check Knowledge check 2
A junior designer brings a polished AI-generated concept but can't explain why it works. How do you coach them?
---
Ask them to evaluate it against stated criteria (hierarchy, grouping, accessibility, consistency, task fit), name the decisions and the alternatives they rejected and test it with a user or colleague. Then have them do part of the next piece without the tool to build the underlying skill. The aim is to build judgment, which tools don't supply.
:::
