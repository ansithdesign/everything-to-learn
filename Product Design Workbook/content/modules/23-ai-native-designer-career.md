The earlier chapters in Part VI are about areas. This one is about you: how the role is changing, which skills compound, how to build a personal system of agents and automations, and how to keep growing when the tools reset every few months.

It brings together [[ch:08]] (collaboration), [[ch:12]] (personal AI practice) and the tool chapters before it.

:::pull
The durable asset is not your tool list. It is your judgment, your taste and your ability to turn a vague problem into something a person or a machine can execute.
:::

## 1. What the evidence says about the role

Treat all of this as signal, not fact:

- **Use is widespread.** Figma's State of the Designer 2026 is reported to show about 72 percent of designers using generative AI and designers' involvement in development doubling to 41 percent;[[ref:figma-sotd-2026]] another survey is cited at an average of seven AI tools per designer.[[ref:fastco-ai-design-jobs]]
- **Hybrid roles are growing.** One job-market tracker, read through a secondary article, reported design-engineering postings up about 14 percent month on month in April 2026 while product-design postings fell about 5 percent.[[ref:hackernoon-middle]] A single tracker over a single month is thin; use it as a prompt to look at postings in your own market.
- **AI-specific design titles are rising.** Secondary sources cite a report ranking "AI UX designer" among the fastest-growing AI titles. I could not check that report directly.
- **Judgment remains the differentiator.** NN/g's State of UX 2026 describes 2026 as a year of AI fatigue and names curated taste, research-informed context, critical thinking and judgment as the harder-to-automate strengths.[[ref:nng-state-ux-2026]]

## 2. Skills that compound

| Skill | Why it compounds | How to build it |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Problem framing** | Models execute; someone must choose the target | Write the problem before opening any tool ([[ch:05]]) |
| **Taste and critique** | Cheap production makes selection the bottleneck | Critique daily against named criteria ([[ch:01]]) |
| **Specification** | Briefs for people and models are the same craft | Practice writing briefs and acceptance criteria |
| **Evaluation** | Knowing if the output is good, not just fast | Build small evals ([[ch:21]]) |
| **Systems thinking** | Systems make AI output consistent | Make your system machine-readable ([[ch:17]]) |
| **Technical literacy** | You can review code, data and agents | Read pull requests; build small things ([[ch:10]]) |
| **Research judgment** | Real users beat synthetic ones | Keep talking to people ([[ch:04]]) |
| **Communication** | Decisions still need people to agree | Write and present clearly ([[ch:07]]) |
| **Ethics and risk** | Responsibility doesn't transfer to the tool | Run risk reviews ([[ch:22]]) |

## 3. Roles and paths

- **Product designer who builds:** prototypes in code, collaborates closely with engineers.
- **Design engineer:** owns the craft of the front end, systems and interaction.
- **AI product designer:** designs behavior, guardrails, evals and trust for AI features ([[ch:20]], [[ch:21]]).
- **Design systems specialist for AI:** makes systems legible to agents.
- **Researcher with AI fluency:** designs studies and verifies machine-assisted analysis.
- **Design lead/strategist:** frames problems, sets standards and governs AI use in the team.

You don't need to pick now, but pick a direction to deepen, and keep one adjacent skill growing.

## 4. Building your personal system

### Context files

Keep a small set of documents you can hand to any model: who you design for, your voice guide, your system rules, your rubric, your standing constraints. Reusing them gives consistent results across tools, and they survive tool changes.

### Prompt and checklist library

Store prompts that worked with the inputs, outputs and a note on when they fail. Keep checklists for review and QA. Date everything.

### Automations and agents

Agents and workflow tools can run tasks such as: summarizing research notes into a weekly brief, checking a Figma file against rules, drafting release notes from changes, or compiling competitor updates. Start with a task you do weekly, keep a human check on outputs, restrict permissions and log what the automation did. MCP connectors make it possible for models to read your files and tools; grant the least access that works.

### Learning loop

Spend a fixed slot each week (an hour) trying one new tool or technique on a real task, scoring it and noting the result in a change log ([[ch:12]]). Drop what doesn't help.

## 5. Working with a team in an AI era

- **Agree norms:** where AI is allowed, what must be disclosed, what data is off limits.
- **Review generated work like any other work**, against the same rubric.
- **Share prompts and findings;** a team library beats private tricks.
- **Be explicit about authorship** in reviews: "I generated this and edited these parts."
- **Mentor juniors on fundamentals.** If tools do the first draft, they need other ways to build judgment; pair them with critique practice.
- **Talk about workload honestly.** Faster production tends to raise expectations rather than free time.

:::warning Common traps
- **Tool-chasing:** switching tools weekly and never getting deep.
- **Skill atrophy:** letting a model do the thinking until you can't without it.
- **Portfolio inflation:** showing polished generated screens with no problem, process or outcome.
- **Hiding AI use** and getting caught, or **over-claiming** it and losing credibility.
- **Waiting for certainty** before experimenting.
:::

## 6. Portfolio and interviews

Show judgment, not just output: the problem, the options, why you chose, what AI did and what you changed, and what happened after. Include one project where you worked with AI openly (a prototype, an eval, a system tweak) and one where you deliberately did not. Be ready to discuss an AI failure you caught. Hiring managers are increasingly asking how you evaluate AI output and how you'd design guardrails.

:::decision A personal AI policy, in one paragraph
Write what you use AI for, what you never give it, how you verify, what you disclose and how you keep practicing without it. Review it every quarter. The ch. 12 template is a good start.
:::

:::exercise Try it: a 90-day plan (2 hours)
Choose a direction from section 3. Write three outcomes for the next ninety days (one skill, one project, one habit). For each, list the evidence you'll collect, the weekly time slot and the AI tool or technique you'll test. Put dated check-ins in your calendar.
:::

## Quick check

:::check Knowledge check 1
A colleague says design jobs are disappearing, so learning to code is the only path. How do you respond?
---
The evidence is mixed and thin: hybrid roles are growing, but judgment, research, systems and AI-behavior design are also in demand. Building technical literacy helps, but it is one route among several. Choose a direction, build evidence in your own market, and keep your judgment sharp.
:::

:::check Knowledge check 2
Which parts of your AI setup should survive a tool change?
---
Your context files, rubrics, checklists, change log, prompt library with notes, and personal AI policy, because they encode your judgment and are portable across tools.
:::
