Two designers pitch the same idea in the same meeting. Designer A walks through a ten-slide double diamond: research, ideation, wireframes, iterations. Designer B opens with "I need a decision today: A or B? Here's the evidence, here's what I recommend, here's what could go wrong."

Same idea. B gets the green light in fifteen minutes. A gets "let's circle back."

Communication isn't a soft add-on to design. It's how your thinking becomes other people's action, and a good designer who explains decisions clearly will often have more influence than a great one who can't. Decisions get made in meetings, documents and chat threads you may not even be in. Your words have to work without you.

We'll cover presenting, writing, critique and facilitation. And we'll deal with something new: how to communicate well when AI can draft anything, and your audience may wonder who made what.

:::pull
In any presentation, document or critique, your job is to make the next decision easier for the other person. If it doesn't, it isn't finished.
:::

## 1. Presenting

### Know what it's for

Every presentation does one of a few jobs: **inform**, **align**, **get a decision**, **get feedback** or **inspire**. Decide which before you open the slides, and say it in the first thirty seconds: "I need a decision today on A vs. B" changes how everyone listens.

### A structure for decisions

1. **Context and goal:** what we're trying to achieve and why now.
2. **The problem, with evidence:** a number, a quote or a short clip.
3. **Options considered:** two or three, with trade-offs.
4. **Recommendation and why,** in terms the audience cares about.
5. **Risks and unknowns,** and how we'd learn.
6. **The ask and next steps:** the decision you need, who does what, by when.

This follows the "answer first" habit, often called the pyramid principle: lead with the conclusion, then support it, instead of making people wait through your journey.

:::framework Present decisions, not process
Portfolio habit says show the whole process. Stakeholders mostly want the **decision and its reasoning**. Show process only where it makes the recommendation credible (the evidence that changed your mind). Keep the full story for people who ask.
:::

### Storytelling with evidence

A story is a person with a goal, an obstacle and a result. Use it to *carry* evidence, not replace it.

- **Combine a number, a quote and a visual.** Number for scale, quote for a person, visual for the problem. Each persuades a different listener.
- **Show a person, not a persona.** Ninety seconds of one participant struggling beats a slide of statistics.
- **Name the stakes.** What happens if we do nothing?
- **Be honest about uncertainty.** Say the strength of the evidence (see [[ch:04]]) and what would change your mind.

### Delivery

Lead with the point. Fewer slides, fewer words (if the slide could be read, put it in a document). Rehearse the first minute and answers to the three hardest questions. Invite disagreement early: "What would make this a bad idea?" Remote: share one link in advance and check in every few minutes.

### 2026 shifts

- **Anyone can make a polished deck.** Polish no longer signals thinking. Clarity of the problem and quality of the recommendation do.
- **Meeting notes and summaries are automatic,** so your words get compressed. Say your key point plainly and repeat it.
- **Say what's generated.** If you include AI-generated imagery, summaries or prototypes, flag it where it matters, especially where the audience could mistake generated content for observed fact.

:::exercise Try it: the two-minute decision brief (30 minutes)
Pick a design decision. Record yourself giving it in two minutes using the six parts above. Play it back and write the one sentence the audience should remember. Can't find it? Rewrite. Then deliver it to a colleague and ask what they'd have asked next.
:::

## 2. Written communication

Teams are more distributed, work is more asynchronous and written records outlast conversations. Good writing scales: one clear memo informs ten people and is still there next quarter.

### Principles

- **Start with what you need.** First line: purpose and ask.
- **One point per paragraph, short sentences, plain words.**
- **Write for someone who wasn't there.**
- **Make it skimmable:** headings, short lists, bold for the decision (not decoration).
- **Link, don't paste,** and say what to look at.

### The documents you'll write

| Document | Purpose | Key contents |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Decision memo | Get or record a decision | Context, options, recommendation, risks, decider |
| Spec or handoff note | Let others build | Behavior, states, edge cases, content, acceptance criteria |
| Project brief | Align before work starts | Problem, users, goals, constraints, success measures, timeline |
| Research summary | Share findings | Question, method, findings with strength, recommendations |
| Status update | Keep people informed | What changed, what's next, what's blocked, what you need |
| Chat message | Ask or answer quickly | A complete, self-contained ask |

:::example A good chat ask
"Can you confirm by Thursday whether the export limit is 10,000 or 50,000 rows? It decides whether we need pagination in the design (draft: link). If you don't know, who would?" It names the question, deadline, reason and fallback, and saves a round trip.
:::

**Async etiquette:** state urgency, use threads, don't send a lone "hi" and wait. When a conversation reaches a decision, write it up and tag the people it affects.

### 2026 shifts

- **First drafts are instant and sameness is common.** Generated text is fluent and generic, and readers notice. Use AI to structure, shorten and check, but write the core argument yourself so it carries your reasoning and voice.
- **Verify facts and numbers.** Models state wrong things confidently, including details of a competitor's product or a statistic.
- **Mind confidentiality.** Don't paste sensitive material into tools you aren't authorized to use.
- **Machines read your docs too.** Specific, well-structured documents are what tools use when they act on your behalf (see [[ch:03]]).

:::exercise Try it: cut a document in half (1 hour)
Take a spec or update you wrote. Rewrite it at half the length without losing a decision, with the ask in the first line. Ask a reader who wasn't involved what they think it's asking for.
:::

## 3. Critique

A design critique is a structured conversation to improve work against its goals. It's not a vote, an approval meeting or a place to share taste. Good critique has four ingredients: **goals**, **specific observations**, **reasoning** and **actionable direction**.

### Structures that help

- **"I like, I wish, what if."** Attributed to Stanford's d.school: say what works, what falls short, and offer an alternative as a question. It makes criticism collaborative and easier to hear.[[ref:i-like-i-wish]]
- **Observation, impact, suggestion.** "Primary and secondary buttons have the same weight (observation), so it's unclear which action moves forward (impact); could the secondary be an outline style (suggestion)?"
- **Actionable vs. preference.** "I don't like green" is preference. "The green on white fails contrast at 3:1" is actionable.
- **Talk about the work, not the person.** Go easy on "you."

### Giving critique

1. Restate the goal first and critique against *it*.
2. Be specific: point to the element.
3. Say the effect on the user or goal.
4. Offer a direction, not a mandate.
5. Ask when unsure ("What led you to this layout?").
6. Say what's working, so people know what to keep.

### Receiving critique

- Listen first, defend later, and write it down.
- Ask clarifying questions. The concern behind a suggestion is often more useful than the suggestion.
- You decide what to act on. Be ready to explain what you didn't take, and why.
- Look for patterns: three people stumbling in the same place matters even if each suggests a different fix.

### Running a good session

State the goal and the *kind* of feedback you want ("only the flow, not the visual style yet"). Share materials beforehand. Give the designer a few minutes to frame it, then let people write silently before discussing so louder voices don't dominate. Capture feedback in one place and end with what the designer will take away.

### 2026 shifts

- **AI critique tools exist.** They can catch inconsistencies, contrast failures and missing states, so they make a fast first pass. They don't know your users or strategy, and they tend to be agreeable. Use them for mechanical checks, never as validation.
- **Critiquing generated work is a core skill.** When a tool makes ten options in a minute, value moves to judging them well. The vocabulary in [[ch:01]] applies directly.
- **Remote critique needs structure.** Written, asynchronous critique with time to think often beats a rushed call.

:::exercise Try it: run a structured critique (30 minutes)
Ask for ten minutes of critique on work in progress. State the goal and the type of feedback you want, use "I like, I wish, what if," then sort feedback into actionable vs. preference and write what you'll change and what you won't, with reasons.
:::

## 4. Facilitation

Facilitation is guiding a group to a result it couldn't easily reach alone, without imposing your own answer. The facilitator owns the **process**, not the content.

### Design the session first

1. **Purpose:** why are we meeting, what will be different after?
2. **Outcome:** a concrete result (ranked list, decision, shared map).
3. **Participants:** who must be there?
4. **Agenda with timing,** alternating **diverging** (many options) and **converging** (choosing).
5. **Methods** that fit the outcome.
6. **Decision rule:** consensus, vote, named decider?
7. **Follow-up:** who does what, by when, where results live.

| Goal | Activities |
| --- | --- |
| Align on a problem | Problem framing, "how might we," assumption mapping |
| Generate ideas | Silent brainstorming, crazy eights, "worst idea" |
| Choose | Dot voting, impact/effort mapping, criteria scoring |
| Understand an experience | Journey mapping, service blueprinting |
| Plan | Story mapping, roadmapping |

The **design sprint**, developed by Jake Knapp and colleagues at Google Ventures, compresses problem definition, sketching, decision, prototyping and testing into one week.[[ref:gv-sprint]] It's great for high-stakes ideas when you can gather the right people, and overused when a smaller exercise would do.

### Techniques that work

- **Silent writing before discussion** equalizes participation and stops anchoring on the first voice.
- **Timebox visibly.**
- **Make quiet people count:** ask directly, use rounds and written input.
- **Park off-topic items** in a visible list.
- **Name the decision rule before the debate,** not after.
- **Redirect dominant voices** kindly: "Thanks, let me hear from others first."

:::warning Common trap: workshops aren't research
A room of colleagues generating ideas builds alignment and energy. It can't produce knowledge about users who aren't in the room. Use workshops to align and generate; use research to verify.
:::

### 2026 shifts

- **Remote and hybrid are normal.** Fewer activities, pre-reads and shorter sessions work better.
- **AI notes reduce the load on a note-taker,** freeing you to facilitate. Review the summary before it circulates, because it can flatten disagreement or misattribute decisions.
- **AI as a participant:** models can widen the option set or play devil's advocate. Use them to broaden, then bring the group's judgment to choose.

:::exercise Try it: plan and run a 60-minute session (half a day)
Pick a real question ("What should we fix first in onboarding?"). Write purpose, outcome, agenda with times, activities and decision rule on one page. Run it with four to six colleagues, then note what you'd change.
:::

## Case: one update, three ways

The same project update can land very differently. Here's a status message about a delayed redesign.

**Version A: the diary.**
> "Hey! Quick update. This week we continued exploring layouts, had a few good sessions with engineering, looked at a bunch of options, and we're still iterating. Should have more soon!"

Nothing to act on, nothing to decide, no sense of risk. The reader learns only that work is happening.

**Version B: the apology.**
> "Sorry, we're running late. There were some unexpected issues and it's taking longer than we thought. We'll try to catch up."

Honest, but it hands the reader a worry without a plan.

**Version C: the useful update.**
> **Status: yellow. Release moves from 12 Nov to 19 Nov unless we cut scope.**
> **Why:** engineering found that the new table can't support inline editing with our current data layer (est. +4 days).
> **Options:** (1) ship with read-only table on 12 Nov, add inline editing in the next release; (2) keep inline editing and ship 19 Nov.
> **Recommendation:** option 1, because 80% of usage in research was read-only.
> **I need from you:** a decision by Thursday so we can plan QA.

Same facts, but C names the status, the cause, the options, a recommendation and the exact ask. A reader can respond in ten seconds.

## A decision memo template

```text
DECISION NEEDED:  [one sentence, with a date]
CONTEXT:          [3 to 5 lines: what's happening and why now]
OPTIONS:
  A. [what it is]  → pros / cons / cost
  B. [what it is]  → pros / cons / cost
RECOMMENDATION:   [A or B, and the reason in one or two lines]
EVIDENCE:         [numbers, quotes, links; label strength]
RISKS / UNKNOWNS: [what could go wrong, how we'd find out]
NEXT STEPS:       [who does what by when once decided]
```

Keep it to one page. If it doesn't fit, you probably haven't decided what you're recommending.

## A deck, slide by slide

For a 15-minute readout of a research study, a tight structure looks like this:

| Slide | Content | Time |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 1 | The decision we need today and what we recommend | 1 min |
| 2 | What we did and how confident we are (method, who, strength of evidence) | 1 min |
| 3 to 5 | Top three findings, each as: claim, evidence (number plus quote or clip), implication | 6 min |
| 6 | What we're *not* sure about | 1 min |
| 7 | Options and recommendation | 2 min |
| 8 | Next steps and owners | 1 min |
| Rest | Discussion | 3 min |

Notice the answer is on slide 1. The rest justifies it.

## Critique phrases

| Instead of... | Say... |
| --- | --- |
| "I don't like it." | "The goal is X; I think this weakens it because Y." |
| "Make it pop." | "Which action should stand out most? Right now two compete." |
| "It's too busy." | "I count five competing focal points; which two matter most?" |
| "This is wrong." | "What led you to this choice? I'd have expected X." |
| "You should..." | "What if we tried...?" |
| "Looks good!" (and nothing else) | "The grouping works well because... one thing I'd test is..." |

## A 90-minute workshop

| Time | Activity | Output |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 0:00 | Frame: goal, constraints, decision rule (5 min) | Shared understanding |
| 0:05 | Review evidence in silence (10 min) | Everyone has read the same facts |
| 0:15 | Silent writing: "problems I see" (10 min) | A pile of individual notes |
| 0:25 | Cluster notes together (15 min) | Named themes |
| 0:40 | Dot vote on themes (5 min) | Ranked list |
| 0:45 | Break (5 min) | |
| 0:50 | For the top three themes: "how might we..." (15 min) | Open-ended questions |
| 1:05 | Impact and effort plotting (15 min) | A shortlist |
| 1:20 | Decide: who owns what, by when (10 min) | Decisions and owners |

Send the outcome to everyone within 24 hours. A workshop that ends without next steps was a very nice meeting.

## Where to go deeper

- **Visual communication:** simple diagrams, annotated screenshots and short walkthrough videos often beat prose.
- **Difficult conversations and negotiation** (see [[ch:08]]).
- **Writing for executives:** short, outcome-oriented, one clear ask.
- **Cross-timezone communication:** plain language and written decisions, without assuming shared context.
- **Communicating AI-assisted work honestly:** disclose when it matters, verify, keep a record.

## Quick check

:::check Knowledge check 1
A stakeholder says "I don't like the layout, make it pop." How do you respond?
---
Ask what they're trying to achieve and what they notice, to turn preference into a goal ("Is the concern that the main action isn't prominent enough?"). Restate the project goal and offer one or two concrete options tied to it. This moves from taste to purpose and gives you something to act on and test.
:::

:::check Knowledge check 2
An AI note-taker's summary says your workshop *decided* something that was only discussed. What do you do?
---
Correct it before it circulates. Check against what actually happened, mark items as decided, open or proposed, name an owner and date, and send the corrected version. Treat AI summaries as drafts: they can flatten disagreement and misattribute decisions.
:::
