Designers often say "the work should speak for itself." It rarely does. A design gets built, changed or killed based on how well people understand the thinking behind it, and that understanding comes from how you present, write, give feedback and run sessions.

This edition explains the foundations of design communication: storytelling, presenting, writing, critique and facilitation.

:::pull
Your design is only as strong as people's understanding of it.
:::

## Why communication matters

Designers communicate with many audiences, each needing something different:

| Audience | What they care about | What to emphasize |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Product managers | Outcomes, scope, timelines | Problem, trade-offs, impact |
| Engineers | Feasibility, details, edge cases | Behavior, states, constraints |
| Executives | Business results, risk | The decision, the evidence, the ask |
| Other designers | Craft, consistency, rationale | Reasoning, system fit |
| Users and stakeholders | Their own needs | Clarity, benefits |

Good communication means adapting the same idea for different people.

## Storytelling

### Why stories work

People remember stories far better than lists of facts. A story gives information a shape: someone wants something, something gets in the way, something changes. That structure helps listeners follow, care and remember.

### Basic story structure

The classic three-act structure fits design presentations well:

1. **Setup:** the context and the person. "Priya manages a team of 12 and needs to know who's overloaded."
2. **Conflict:** the problem. "Today she checks five tools and still guesses."
3. **Resolution:** the change your design creates. "With the workload view, she sees it at a glance and rebalances in minutes."

### Other useful structures

- **Situation, Complication, Resolution (SCR):** from consulting; quick and logical.
- **Before, After, Bridge:** what life is like now, what it could be, how your design gets there.
- **The hero's journey (simplified):** the user is the hero; your product is the guide, not the hero.

### Making data persuasive

Combine three kinds of evidence:

- **A number** shows scale ("40% abandon at this step").
- **A quote** shows a person ("I just gave up and called support").
- **A visual or clip** shows the problem happening.

Each convinces a different kind of listener.

## Presenting design work

### Know your goal

Before any presentation, decide what you need:

- **Inform:** share progress or results.
- **Get feedback:** on specific parts.
- **Align:** get everyone on the same page.
- **Get a decision:** choose between options.

Say it at the start: "Today I need a decision between these two directions."

### A reliable structure

1. **Goal and context:** what we're solving and why.
2. **Users and problem:** who is affected, with evidence.
3. **Approach:** what you explored, briefly.
4. **The design:** walk through it as a user would experience it.
5. **Rationale:** why this solution, linked to goals and evidence.
6. **Trade-offs and open questions.**
7. **Next steps and the ask.**

### The pyramid principle

Barbara Minto's **pyramid principle** says: lead with the main point, then the supporting arguments, then the details. Busy audiences get the answer immediately and can dig deeper if they want.

### Presenting tips

- Walk through flows from the user's point of view, not screen by screen.
- Use real content, not "lorem ipsum."
- Show the important states (empty, error), not just the perfect one.
- Explain *why*, not just *what*.
- Prepare for the hardest three questions.
- Pause and invite questions; silence is fine.
- Remote: share links in advance, keep slides simple and check in often.

### Handling tough feedback

- **Listen fully** before responding.
- **Ask clarifying questions:** "What concerns you about it?"
- **Connect back to goals:** "We chose this because it reduces errors, which was our main goal."
- **It's okay to say** "Good point, let me look into it."

## Written communication

### Why writing matters

Teams increasingly work across time zones and asynchronously. Clear writing lets decisions happen without meetings and leaves a record.

### Principles of clear writing

1. **Lead with the point.** State the main message or ask first.
2. **Know your reader.** What do they already know? What do they need?
3. **Use plain language.** Short sentences, common words, no jargon.
4. **Structure for scanning.** Headings, bullets, bold key phrases.
5. **Be specific.** "Increase setup completion from 40% to 50%" beats "improve onboarding."
6. **Edit ruthlessly.** Most first drafts can lose a third of their words.

### Common design documents

| Document | Purpose | Typical contents |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Design brief | Align before starting | Problem, goals, users, constraints, success metrics |
| Design spec / handoff | Explain how something works | Flows, states, behavior, edge cases |
| Research report | Share findings | Questions, method, findings, recommendations |
| Decision record | Record why a choice was made | Context, options, decision, reasoning |
| Release notes | Tell users what changed | What's new, why it helps |
| Status update | Keep people informed | Progress, risks, next steps, needs |

### Chat and email

- Make messages **self-contained**: include context, the question and any deadline.
- Don't just say "hi" and wait; ask your question directly.
- Use threads to keep conversations organized.
- Summarize decisions in writing so everyone knows what was agreed.

### UX writing vs. design writing

**UX writing** (or content design) is the text inside the product: buttons, labels, error messages, onboarding. **Design writing** is how you communicate about your work. Both use the same core principle: clarity first.

## Critique

### What critique is (and isn't)

A **design critique** is a structured conversation to improve a design against its goals. It is *not*:

- A vote on what people like.
- An approval meeting.
- A place to redesign someone's work for them.

### Roles in a critique

- **Presenter:** shares the work, the goals and what feedback is needed.
- **Critics:** give focused, constructive feedback.
- **Facilitator:** keeps time, keeps the discussion on track and makes sure everyone is heard.

### Giving good feedback

Good feedback is:

- **Tied to goals:** "Since the goal is speed, the extra step might slow people down."
- **Specific:** point to the exact element.
- **Explained:** say *why* it matters.
- **Actionable:** suggest a direction, not a mandate.
- **Balanced:** mention what works as well as what doesn't.

Useful formats:

- **I like, I wish, what if:** what works, what could be better, an idea to explore.[[ref:i-like-i-wish]]
- **Observation, impact, suggestion:** what you see, why it matters, what might help.
- **Question first:** "What led you to this layout?" before suggesting changes.

### Actionable vs. preference

| Preference (less useful) | Actionable (more useful) |
| --- | --- |
| "I don't like the blue." | "The blue link has low contrast against the gray background." |
| "It feels cluttered." | "Five elements compete for attention at the top; the main action gets lost." |
| "Make it pop." | "The primary action doesn't stand out from the secondary ones." |

### Receiving feedback well

- **Listen without defending** at first.
- **Take notes.**
- **Ask questions** to understand the concern behind a suggestion.
- **Separate yourself from the work:** feedback is about the design, not you.
- **You decide what to act on,** but explain your reasoning.

## Facilitation and workshops

### What facilitation is

**Facilitation** is guiding a group to reach a goal together. The facilitator manages the *process* (time, activities, participation), not the *content*.

### Planning a workshop

1. **Purpose:** why are we meeting?
2. **Outcome:** what will we have at the end (a list, a decision, a map)?
3. **Participants:** who needs to be there?
4. **Agenda:** activities with timings.
5. **Materials:** boards, templates, pre-reads.
6. **Follow-up:** how results will be shared and used.

### Diverge, then converge

Good workshops alternate between:

- **Diverging:** generating many ideas without judging.
- **Converging:** evaluating, grouping and choosing.

Mixing the two at the same time ("let's brainstorm, but only good ideas") kills creativity.

### Common workshop activities

| Activity | What it does |
| --- | --- |
| Icebreaker | Warms up the group |
| How might we | Turns problems into opportunities |
| Crazy 8s | Each person sketches 8 ideas in 8 minutes |
| Affinity mapping | Groups ideas or findings into themes |
| Dot voting | Each person gets a few votes to prioritize |
| Impact/effort matrix | Prioritizes ideas visually |
| Journey mapping | Maps a user's experience together |
| Lightning demos | Quick sharing of inspiring examples |
| Rose, thorn, bud | Reflection: what's good, bad, promising |

### Design sprints

The **design sprint**, created at Google Ventures, is a five-day process to answer a critical question through design, prototyping and testing:[[ref:gv-sprint]]

| Day | Focus |
| --- | --- |
| Monday | Map the problem and pick a target |
| Tuesday | Sketch competing solutions |
| Wednesday | Decide on the best ideas |
| Thursday | Build a realistic prototype |
| Friday | Test with real users |

It works best for big, risky questions where a small team can commit fully for a week.

### Facilitation techniques

- **Silent work first:** individuals write ideas before discussing, which prevents the loudest voice from dominating.
- **Timeboxing:** keep activities to fixed times.
- **Parking lot:** capture off-topic ideas for later.
- **Round robin:** go around so everyone speaks.
- **Clear decision rules:** decide in advance how decisions are made (vote, consensus, decider).

### Remote facilitation

- Use a shared online whiteboard.
- Send instructions and links in advance.
- Keep sessions shorter and take breaks.
- Use chat and reactions for quieter participants.
- Summarize outcomes in writing afterwards.

## Key terms

| Term | Plain meaning |
| --- | --- |
| Pyramid principle | Lead with the main point, then support it |
| SCR | Situation, Complication, Resolution |
| Design critique | Structured feedback against goals |
| Actionable feedback | Specific, goal-linked, explained |
| Facilitation | Guiding a group's process toward a goal |
| Diverge / converge | Generate ideas / narrow them down |
| Dot voting | Simple group prioritization |
| Design sprint | A five-day process to test an idea |
| Decision record | A written record of a decision and why |

## Quick check

:::check Knowledge check 1
An executive has five minutes. How should you structure your update?
---
Lead with the answer or decision you need (pyramid principle), then the key evidence and impact, then risks and next steps. Keep details available if they ask.
:::

:::check Knowledge check 2
Turn "I don't like this page" into actionable feedback.
---
Make it specific and goal-linked, for example: "Since the goal is to get users to upgrade, the upgrade button is hard to find because it looks the same as the secondary links. Could it be more prominent?"
:::

:::check Knowledge check 3
Why do workshops separate diverging from converging?
---
Judging ideas while generating them makes people self-censor and reduces creativity. Generating first and evaluating later produces more and better options.
:::
