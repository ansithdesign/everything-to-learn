You can be the most talented designer on the team and still see your work watered down, delayed or ignored. Product design is a team sport. Designs get built through engineers, prioritized by product managers, approved by stakeholders and improved by everyone's input.

This edition explains the foundations of collaboration and leadership for designers: how cross-functional teams work, how to manage stakeholders, how to mentor and grow others, and how groups make good decisions.

:::pull
Your influence comes less from your title than from how well you help other people make good decisions.
:::

## Cross-functional collaboration

### What a product team looks like

Most product teams include:

| Role | Main responsibility | How designers work with them |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Product manager (PM) | What to build and why; priorities | Shape problems, define success together |
| Engineer | How to build it; technical quality | Feasibility, details, edge cases |
| Designer | How it works and looks for users | Research, flows, interaction, visuals |
| Researcher | Understanding users | Plan studies, share insights |
| Content designer | Words in the product | Labels, messages, tone |
| Data analyst | Measuring behavior | Define metrics, read results |
| QA / test engineer | Quality before release | Test cases, edge cases |

### The product trio

Many teams center on a **trio**: product manager, designer and tech lead. They share ownership of discovery and decisions, which leads to better ideas and fewer surprises.

### How teams work: agile basics

Most software teams use **agile** methods: work happens in short cycles with frequent feedback.

- **Scrum:** work is planned in **sprints** (usually 1 to 4 weeks), with rituals like sprint planning, daily stand-ups, reviews and retrospectives.
- **Kanban:** work flows continuously across a board (To do → In progress → Done), with limits on work in progress.
- **User stories:** short descriptions of needs: "As a [user], I want [goal], so that [benefit]."
- **Backlog:** a prioritized list of work.
- **Definition of done:** what must be true for work to count as finished.

Designers usually work slightly **ahead** of engineering (designing next sprint's work) while supporting the current sprint.

### Principles of good collaboration

1. **Involve others early.** Show engineers rough ideas before polishing.
2. **Share problems, not just solutions.** People who understand *why* contribute better ideas.
3. **Be clear about roles and decisions.** Who decides what?
4. **Over-communicate.** Share work in progress openly.
5. **Respect constraints.** Ask why something is hard instead of assuming people are being difficult.
6. **Give credit generously.**

### Working well with engineers

- Learn the basics of how your product is built.
- Walk engineers through designs before handoff, not just after.
- Design all states and edge cases.
- Be available during the build; questions will come up.
- Review the built product together and fix differences collaboratively.

### Working with PMs

- Align on the problem and success metrics before designing.
- Bring research and evidence, not just opinions.
- Discuss trade-offs openly (scope, time, quality).
- Help PMs communicate the user's perspective to stakeholders.

## Stakeholder management

### Who stakeholders are

**Stakeholders** are anyone who affects or is affected by your work: executives, other teams, sales, support, legal, marketing and sometimes customers.

### Stakeholder mapping

A common tool is the **power/interest grid**:

| | Low interest | High interest |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **High power** | Keep satisfied | Manage closely |
| **Low power** | Monitor | Keep informed |

Plot your stakeholders, then decide how often and how deeply to involve each.

### RACI: who does what

**RACI** clarifies roles for a task or decision:

- **R**esponsible: does the work.
- **A**ccountable: owns the outcome (one person).
- **C**onsulted: gives input before decisions.
- **I**nformed: kept updated.

### Managing expectations

- **Align early:** agree on goals, scope and timeline at the start.
- **Share progress regularly:** no surprises.
- **Explain trade-offs:** "If we add this, the release moves two weeks."
- **Flag risks early.**
- **Document decisions.**

### Handling disagreement

1. **Listen** to understand their concern.
2. **Find the underlying goal:** what are they really trying to achieve?
3. **Bring evidence:** research, data, examples.
4. **Offer options** with trade-offs.
5. **Agree on how to decide:** test it, ask the decider or prototype both.
6. **Disagree and commit:** once decided, support the decision.

### Saying no constructively

Instead of "no," try: "Yes, if..." or "Not now, because..." For example: "We can add that, but it would delay launch by two weeks. Should we prioritize it over X, or plan it for the next release?"

## Mentoring and developing others

### Why it matters

Helping others grow multiplies your impact, strengthens the team and is expected as you become more senior. It also deepens your own understanding: teaching forces clarity.

### Mentor, coach or manage?

| Approach | What it means |
| --- | --- |
| Mentoring | Sharing experience and advice |
| Coaching | Asking questions to help someone find their own answers |
| Sponsoring | Using your influence to create opportunities for someone |
| Managing | Responsibility for someone's work, performance and growth |

### Good mentoring practices

- **Start with their goals**, not yours for them.
- **Ask more than you tell.** "What options do you see?"
- **Give specific feedback:** what they did, its effect, what to try next.
- **Share your reasoning,** not just your answers.
- **Give stretch opportunities** with support.
- **Celebrate progress.**
- **Be consistent:** regular check-ins beat occasional long sessions.

### The feedback model: SBI

A simple way to give feedback:

- **Situation:** "In yesterday's review..."
- **Behavior:** "...you explained the trade-offs clearly with data..."
- **Impact:** "...which helped the PM decide quickly."

It works for both positive and constructive feedback.

### Growth plans

A simple growth plan includes: current strengths, two or three areas to develop, specific actions, support needed, and a time to review.

### Career frameworks

Many companies use **career ladders** that describe expectations at each level (junior, mid, senior, staff) across skills like craft, collaboration, impact and leadership. Understanding your company's ladder helps you and your mentees know what growth looks like.

## Leadership without authority

You don't need a manager title to lead. Leadership means helping a group move forward. Designers lead by:

- **Framing problems** so everyone understands them.
- **Bringing evidence** that changes minds.
- **Making things visible:** prototypes, maps and diagrams align people quickly.
- **Facilitating** discussions and decisions.
- **Setting standards** through the quality of their own work.
- **Following through** on commitments.

### The IC and manager paths

| Individual contributor (IC) | Manager |
| --- | --- |
| Deep craft and expertise | People development and team health |
| Leads through work and influence | Leads through people and process |
| Senior, staff, principal designer | Design manager, head of design, director |

Both are valid leadership paths.

## Group decision-making

### Common decision methods

| Method | How it works | Best when |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Consensus | Everyone agrees | Small groups, high-stakes alignment |
| Consent | Proceed unless someone has a serious objection | Teams that need speed and inclusion |
| Majority vote | Most votes win | Many options, low risk |
| Decider after input | One person decides after hearing everyone | Clear ownership, speed |
| Delegation | Hand the decision to a person or team | Expertise sits elsewhere |

### DACI

Another role framework for decisions:

- **D**river: drives the process.
- **A**pprover: makes the final call.
- **C**ontributors: give input.
- **I**nformed: told the outcome.

### Avoiding common traps

- **HiPPO** (highest paid person's opinion) dominating: bring evidence and structured methods.
- **Groupthink:** invite dissent; ask "what would make this fail?"
- **Endless debate:** set a deadline and a decision rule.
- **Unclear outcomes:** write down what was decided and who owns next steps.

### Team health

Google's Project Aristotle research found **psychological safety** (feeling safe to take risks and speak up) was the most important factor in effective teams. Leaders build it by admitting mistakes, inviting input and responding to ideas with curiosity rather than judgment.

## Quick check

:::check Knowledge check 1
A stakeholder has high power but low interest in your project. How should you involve them?
---
Keep them satisfied: brief, occasional updates focused on what matters to them, and involve them more closely if decisions start to affect their area.
:::

:::check Knowledge check 2
Rewrite this feedback using SBI: "Good job on the presentation."
---
"In today's stakeholder review (situation), you opened with the decision and backed it with the usability data (behavior), which got us a yes in ten minutes (impact)."
:::

:::check Knowledge check 3
What's the difference between consensus and consent?
---
Consensus requires everyone to agree. Consent means the group proceeds unless someone has a serious, reasoned objection, which is faster while still respecting concerns.
:::
