A great interface that doesn't help the business survive won't be around long, and neither will the team that built it. Product designers who understand how the business works make better decisions, get invited into earlier conversations and can explain why their work matters.

This edition explains the basics from the ground up: what product thinking is, how companies make money, the metrics that matter, how teams prioritize, how to measure design's impact and how to position your work.

:::pull
Design → product → business → outcome. A strong product designer can trace a line through all four.
:::

## Product thinking

### What it is

**Product thinking** is approaching design as a way to solve real problems that create value for users *and* the business. It means asking:

- **Who** is this for, and what problem do they have?
- **Why** does it matter to them, and to us?
- **What** outcome are we trying to change?
- **How** will we know if it worked?
- **What** should we *not* build?

A UI designer focused only on the screen asks, "How should this look?" A product thinker first asks, "Should this exist, and what should it achieve?"

### The product triangle

Good products sit where three things overlap:

- **Desirable:** users want it.
- **Viable:** the business can sustain it.
- **Feasible:** the team can build it.

Designers are often strongest on desirability. Product thinking means caring about the other two as well.

### Features vs. outcomes

A **feature** is something the product does. An **outcome** is a change in what users do (or in business results). Teams that celebrate shipping features ("we launched 12 things!") without checking outcomes often build a lot that nobody uses.

### The product lifecycle

Products go through stages, and good design decisions differ by stage:

| Stage | Focus | Design priority |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Introduction | Finding product–market fit | Learning fast, core value |
| Growth | Acquiring and activating users | Onboarding, scalability |
| Maturity | Retaining and expanding | Efficiency, depth, polish |
| Decline | Managing or reinventing | Simplification, migration |

## How businesses make money

### Business models

| Model | How it works | Design implications |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Subscription (SaaS) | Recurring monthly or yearly fee | Retention is everything; onboarding and ongoing value matter |
| Freemium | Free tier plus paid upgrades | Clear upgrade moments without annoying free users |
| Usage-based | Pay for what you use | Usage visibility, limits, cost predictability |
| Transactional | Fee or margin per sale | Conversion and trust at checkout |
| Marketplace | Commission between buyers and sellers | Trust and value for both sides |
| Advertising | Free to users, paid by advertisers | Engagement, balanced against user trust |
| License / one-time | Pay once | Strong first impression, upgrade paths |

### B2B vs. B2C

- **B2C (business to consumer):** individuals choose, pay and use. Emotional appeal and simplicity matter a lot.
- **B2B (business to business):** companies buy for their employees. The **buyer** (who pays), the **decision-maker** (who approves) and the **user** (who uses it daily) are often different people. Admin tools, permissions, security and integrations matter as much as the core experience.

### Go-to-market

- **Product-led growth (PLG):** users discover, try and adopt the product themselves (free trials, freemium). Design drives growth.
- **Sales-led growth:** sales teams find and close customers, often with demos. Design supports trust, evaluation and onboarding after purchase.

### Unit economics basics

- **CAC (customer acquisition cost):** how much it costs to win a customer.
- **LTV (lifetime value):** how much revenue a customer brings over time.
- A healthy business usually needs LTV comfortably higher than CAC.
- **Gross margin:** revenue minus the direct cost of delivering the product.

Design affects all of these: clear value lowers CAC (more conversions), and good experiences raise LTV (customers stay longer).

## Metrics and analytics literacy

### Why metrics matter for designers

Metrics turn "I think this is better" into "this improved completion by 8 percent." They help you find problems, prioritize and prove impact.

### Common product metrics

| Metric | What it measures |
| --- | --- |
| Acquisition | How many new users or signups arrive |
| Activation | How many reach a first meaningful moment of value |
| Retention | How many keep coming back over time |
| Churn | How many leave (the opposite of retention) |
| Engagement | How often and how deeply people use the product |
| Conversion rate | Percentage who complete a goal (sign up, buy) |
| ARPU | Average revenue per user |
| MRR / ARR | Monthly or annual recurring revenue |
| NPS | Net Promoter Score: how likely users are to recommend |
| CSAT | Customer satisfaction score |
| Task success / time on task | Usability measures |

### Frameworks for choosing metrics

**AARRR ("pirate metrics")** by Dave McClure tracks the customer lifecycle: **Acquisition, Activation, Retention, Referral, Revenue.**

**HEART** from Google is designed for user experience:

| Dimension | Example signal |
| --- | --- |
| Happiness | Satisfaction surveys |
| Engagement | Sessions per week |
| Adoption | New users of a feature |
| Retention | Users still active after 30 days |
| Task success | Completion rate, errors |

HEART is paired with **Goals → Signals → Metrics**: define the goal, the behavior that would show it, then the number you'll track.

**North Star metric:** one key metric that best captures the value users get (for example "weekly active teams creating projects"). It aligns teams around one direction.

### Reading data

- **Funnels** show the steps to a goal and where people drop off.
- **Cohorts** group users by when they started, to compare behavior over time.
- **Retention curves** show what percentage of a cohort is still active over time. A curve that flattens means some users found lasting value.
- **Segments** split data by user type, plan, device or region.

### Leading vs. lagging indicators

- **Lagging** metrics show results after the fact (revenue, churn).
- **Leading** metrics predict them earlier (activation, weekly engagement).

Design work usually moves leading metrics first.

### Avoiding common mistakes

- **Correlation isn't causation.** Two things moving together doesn't mean one caused the other.
- **Vanity metrics** (total signups, page views) look good but don't show real value.
- **Averages hide differences.** Look at segments and distributions.
- **Small samples are noisy.**

### A/B testing basics

An **A/B test** shows version A to one random group and version B to another, then compares a metric. Key ideas: define a hypothesis and primary metric first, run until you have enough data, and don't stop the test the moment it looks good. A test tells you *which* version performed better, not *why*.

## Prioritization frameworks

| Framework | How it works | Good for |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Impact / effort matrix | Plot on a 2×2: quick wins, big bets, fill-ins, money pits | Fast team alignment |
| RICE | (Reach × Impact × Confidence) ÷ Effort | Comparing many ideas |
| ICE | Impact × Confidence × Ease | Fast scoring of experiments |
| MoSCoW | Must, Should, Could, Won't | Scoping a release |
| Kano | Basic, performance and delight features | Understanding user expectations |
| Cost of delay | What waiting costs | Time-sensitive decisions |
| Opportunity scoring | Importance vs. current satisfaction | Finding underserved needs |

### How RICE works

- **Reach:** how many people it affects in a time period (for example 500 users per month).
- **Impact:** how much it affects each person (often scored 0.25, 0.5, 1, 2, 3).
- **Confidence:** how sure you are (100%, 80%, 50%).
- **Effort:** person-months of work.

Score = (Reach × Impact × Confidence) ÷ Effort. Higher means a better bet. The value is less in the number and more in discussing the assumptions behind it.

### The Kano model

| Category | If present | If absent |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Basic (must-be) | Expected, little delight | Strong dissatisfaction |
| Performance | More is better | Less is worse |
| Delighters | Pleasant surprise | Not missed |

Over time, delighters become expected basics (think of how mobile check-in moved from novelty to standard).

## Design ROI and impact

### Why measure design's impact

Design is often seen as a cost. Showing its impact helps secure investment, prioritize work and earn a seat in decisions.

### What design can improve

- **Revenue:** higher conversion, upgrades, retention.
- **Cost:** fewer support tickets, less rework, faster development with a design system.
- **Efficiency:** time saved for users (especially in internal and B2B tools).
- **Risk:** fewer errors, better accessibility compliance.
- **Satisfaction:** higher NPS, CSAT and usability scores.

### A simple way to calculate impact

1. **Baseline:** measure before the change (for example, 40% complete setup).
2. **Change:** measure after (for example, 46%).
3. **Translate to value:** 6 extra points × 1,000 trials a month = 60 more activated users; if 25% of activated users pay $100 a month, that's 15 new customers, or $1,500 a month in new recurring revenue (illustrative numbers).
4. **Compare with cost:** design and build time.
5. **Be honest about attribution:** what else changed at the same time?

### Evidence from industry

McKinsey's 2018 "Business Value of Design" research analyzed about 300 public companies and reported that those with strong design practices outperformed industry benchmarks on growth.[[ref:mckinsey-design]] It's widely cited, but it shows correlation, not proof that design caused the growth, so use it as supporting context rather than your main argument.

### UX maturity

Organizations differ in how much they value and use design. NN/g describes stages from "absent" (UX is ignored) to "user-driven" (UX is part of every decision). Knowing where your organization is helps you choose realistic goals.

## Strategic positioning

### Positioning your work

Present your work in terms of problems and outcomes, not just deliverables:

- Weak: "I redesigned the settings page."
- Strong: "Support tickets about permissions were our top category; I redesigned the permissions page, and tickets dropped by about a third."

### Positioning yourself

As you grow, your scope expands:

| Level | Typical scope |
| --- | --- |
| Junior | Tasks and screens, with guidance |
| Mid-level | Features and flows, independently |
| Senior | Problem areas across features; shapes direction |
| Staff / principal | Product areas or cross-team problems; influences strategy |

Positioning yourself means being clear about the problems you solve, the impact you've had and the kind of work you want to grow into.

## Quick check

:::check Knowledge check 1
Total signups went up 50%, but revenue didn't change. What might be happening?
---
Signups may be a vanity metric here. New users might not be activating or converting to paid. Look at activation, retention and conversion for the new signups, and check where the extra traffic came from.
:::

:::check Knowledge check 2
In B2B, why might a beautiful experience for daily users still fail to win a deal?
---
Because the buyer and decision-maker are often different people with different needs (security, admin controls, integrations, price). The product has to satisfy them too.
:::

:::check Knowledge check 3
A feature scores high on reach and impact but has 30% confidence. What should you do?
---
Low confidence means you're mostly guessing. Before building, run discovery or a cheap test to raise confidence. Then re-score.
:::
